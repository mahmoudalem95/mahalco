Three complete workflowsOpen the full sequences

Not a single tool, but the path from raw input to the thing you submit. Every step links to the tool that performs it.

From turning movements to level of service and target years

Input: peak hour turning movements. Output: delay and level of service now, and an LOS matrix for the forecast years.

  1. Critical volumeEnter the twelve movements in critical lane volume; three phasing methods are computed in parallel and the lowest is selected.
  2. Intersection LOSPost the same inputs to signalized intersection analysis for delay, v/c and LOS per approach.
  3. Queues and baysLane and queue analysis gives the 95th percentile queue against the turn bay you actually drew.

Counts are entered movement by movement. A direct import of the Israeli counts spreadsheet exists in the Hebrew edition of the site.

Corridor rather than intersection

Input: a sequence of signals along an arterial. Output: travel speed, level of service and a coordination plan for the corridor.

  1. SegmentUrban street segment analysis for running speed and LOS between two signals.
  2. FacilityUrban street facility analysis aggregates consecutive segments into one corridor result.
  3. CoordinationSignal coordination and timing for offsets, splits and a common cycle length.

Segment results depend heavily on the assumed arrival type — state it explicitly in any submission.

Freeway and interchange, end to end

Input: mainline and ramp volumes with a geometric layout. Output: density, level of service and reliability across the whole facility.

  1. Basic segmentsBasic freeway segment analysis for density and LOS on uninterrupted sections.
  2. Ramps and weavingMerge and diverge plus weaving segment analysis at the junctions.
  3. FacilityFreeway facility analysis combines the segments into one LOS for the corridor.
  4. ReliabilityTravel time reliability shows how the facility behaves on a bad day, not an average one.

For the interchange itself, interchange LOS covers the ramp terminals.

Why chaining the tools is the interesting part

One set of counts feeds an entire sequence. Each link adds a layer that normally means copying numbers between programs by hand — and every such copy is a chance to be wrong.

Counts→ Intersection LOS→ Signal timing→ Queues→ Sight distance
  • The peak hour is established once and serves every check downstream.
  • Queue length is tested against the turn bay that was actually designed.
  • The growth forecast reruns the whole sequence for each target year.
  • Heavy vehicle share and PCU conversion are applied once, not per tool.

Today the sequence runs by manual hand-off between tools. A shared data layer is in development — want early access?

Frequently asked questionsOpen the Q&A

Short answers to questions that arrive by email and in search.

Which standards do the calculations follow?
HCM for capacity, level of service, delay and queues; the AASHTO Green Book for geometric design; the HSM for safety performance. Where an Israeli guideline governs — parking regulations, urban street guidelines, interurban design guidelines, roundabout guidelines — the tool follows it and says so.
Do I need to install anything or upload my files?
No. Every tool runs in the browser, including reading a counts spreadsheet and marking up a plan. Nothing is uploaded to a server. The API exists separately, for embedding the same calculations in your own systems.
What is the difference between the in-browser pages and the API?
Identical methods, different interface. The pages are for a single intersection, segment or cross-section; the API is for batches — a corridor, a scenario matrix, or a nightly run against your own database.
How is a future-year level of service produced?
Every movement is grown at a compound annual rate and the whole chain is recomputed — critical volume, cycle length, green split, delay. The delay is never simply multiplied by the growth factor, because delay is not linear in volume and diverges around a volume-to-capacity ratio of 1.0.
Do the results replace a signed traffic report?
No. The tools perform the calculation to the standard and expose the intermediate steps so they can be checked. A traffic impact assessment, a safety report or a submission to an authority requires a licensed traffic engineer who takes professional responsibility for the assumptions and the result.
